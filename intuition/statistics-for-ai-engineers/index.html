The Only Statistics You Need to Become an AI Engineer

Not a prerequisite checklist. This is what's already running under your model - explained via the 20% of each concept that does 80% of the work.

Most "statistics for ML" content teaches it as a gate you have to pass before the real work starts. That's backwards. The concepts below aren't prerequisites - they're descriptions of what your model is already doing, whether you've studied them or not. This is the Pareto cut of each one: the small slice that explains almost everything you'll actually touch as an AI engineer, plus exactly where it shows up in code you run.

1. Expectation, Variance, Covariance

The three numbers that summarize any distribution: where it's centered (expectation), how spread out it is (variance), and how two variables move together (covariance).

The Pareto Cut Skip the formal proofs. The only intuition you need: expectation is a weighted average, variance is "how wrong is the average as a summary," and covariance is "do these two things go up together, down together, or independently."
Where It Shows Up Batch normalization subtracts the batch mean and divides by batch variance - literally this concept, live, in every forward pass. Covariance is the backbone of PCA and why embedding dimensions end up correlated with each other.
Learn From Any intro probability course covers this well - the goal here is fluency, not a novel source.

2. Probability Distributions

Normal, Bernoulli, Binomial - the shapes that describe how likely different outcomes are.

The Pareto Cut You need exactly one mental model: softmax turns a vector of numbers into a probability distribution over your vocabulary. Everything downstream - temperature, top-p, top-k - is just different ways of reshaping or truncating that distribution before sampling from it.
Where It Shows Up Every single token an LLM generates is a sample from a categorical distribution. "Temperature 0.7" is not a magic knob - it's dividing the logits before softmax, which flattens or sharpens that distribution.
Learn From DeepLearning.AI's "Probability & Statistics for Machine Learning" (Coursera); 3Blue1Brown's probability series for visual intuition.

3. Bayes' Theorem

How to update a belief (the posterior) given a prior belief and new evidence (the likelihood).

The Pareto Cut Forget memorizing the formula. Internalize the sentence: "how much should this new evidence change what I already believed?" If your prior was strong and the new evidence is weak, don't overreact to it. That's the entire idea.
Where It Shows Up Bayesian hyperparameter search (used by Optuna and similar tools) builds a probabilistic model of which hyperparameters are likely to work well, updating as each trial completes. It's also the correct mental model for treating an LLM's output confidence as one signal among several, not gospel.
Learn From Stanford CS109 lecture notes (free, online); the "Learning Bayesian Statistics" podcast for applied intuition.

4. Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE)

Finding the model parameters that make your observed training data the most probable outcome.

The Pareto Cut You don't need the calculus. You need the sentence: training a model IS maximum likelihood estimation. Every gradient descent step nudges parameters toward assigning higher probability to the data you actually have.
Where It Shows Up This is the theoretical justification for why minimizing cross-entropy loss (see #5) trains a language model at all - cross-entropy loss is the negative log-likelihood, and minimizing it is MLE in practice.
Learn From Stanford CS229 notes (Andrew Ng); the MLE chapter in Goodfellow, Bengio, and Courville's "Deep Learning."

5. Cross-Entropy, KL Divergence, and Perplexity

Three related ways of measuring the gap between what your model predicts and what's actually true.

The Pareto Cut Cross-entropy is the negative log-probability your model assigned to the correct answer. KL divergence is mathematically the same objective, viewed as "the distance between the model's distribution and reality." Perplexity is just cross-entropy exponentiated - a different unit, same idea. You genuinely only need to understand one of the three deeply; the other two are re-labelings.
Where It Shows Up Cross-entropy is the literal loss function training every LLM being trained right now. KL divergence is the constraint term in RLHF that stops a fine-tuned model from drifting too far from its reference policy - it's why your model doesn't forget how to talk after alignment training. Perplexity is the classic pre-benchmark-era way papers reported "how good" a language model was.
Learn From Eli Bendersky's "Cross-entropy and KL divergence" writeup; Karpathy's from-scratch GPT build (watch him implement this loss function directly).

6. Bias-Variance Tradeoff

The tension between a model being too simple to capture the pattern (high bias) and too sensitive to the specific training data it saw (high variance).

The Pareto Cut One picture is enough: high bias = underfitting = the model is too dumb for the task. High variance = overfitting = the model memorized noise instead of signal. Every regularization technique you'll ever use is trying to move you along this tradeoff, not eliminate it.
Where It Shows Up This is the actual theoretical reason dropout, weight decay, and early stopping work - they all deliberately add a bit of bias to reduce variance, trading a small amount of training-set fit for better generalization.
Learn From Andrew Ng's Machine Learning Specialization - still the clearest treatment of this tradeoff available for free.

7. Central Limit Theorem & Confidence Intervals

Why averages of samples tend toward a normal distribution, and how to express uncertainty around a measured number.

The Pareto Cut The one number you need to internalize: sample size drives trust. A 20-example eval "win" and a 2,000-example eval "win" are not the same claim, even if both show the identical percentage improvement. Small samples produce noisy, overconfident-looking results.
Where It Shows Up Every time you compare two prompts, two models, or two RAG configurations on a small eval set and declare a winner, this is the concept that tells you whether that declaration is trustworthy or noise.
Learn From Khan Academy's statistics course for the intuition, then apply it directly the next time you size an eval set.

8. Hypothesis Testing / A-B Testing

A formal framework for deciding whether an observed difference is real or could plausibly be chance.

The Pareto Cut You don't need to memorize test statistics by name. You need the workflow: decide your sample size and what you're measuring in advance, run it, look once. The single most damaging mistake is "peeking" - checking results early and stopping as soon as it looks good, which inflates your false-positive rate dramatically (from an intended 10% up to roughly 55% in documented cases).
Where It Shows Up This is how you should be validating that a new prompt, a new model version, or a new RAG pipeline change is actually better in production - not by eyeballing a handful of examples and declaring victory.
Learn From GrowthBook and Statsig's engineering blogs - both are refreshingly practical rather than textbook-abstract.

9. Precision, Recall, F1, ROC-AUC

The vocabulary for describing how good a classifier, guardrail, or retrieval system actually is.

The Pareto Cut Accuracy lies on imbalanced data. If 99% of requests are benign, a classifier that always says "benign" is 99% accurate and completely useless. Precision (of what I flagged, how much was real) and recall (of what was real, how much did I catch) are the two numbers that actually matter, and they trade off against each other.
Where It Shows Up Every guardrail, every content filter, and every retrieval system in a RAG pipeline should be reported on precision/recall, not accuracy. If someone hands you a single accuracy number for an imbalanced problem, ask what the class balance was before you trust it.
Learn From Google's Machine Learning Crash Course, classification metrics module (free).

10. Distribution Shift

When the real-world data a deployed model sees starts to differ from the data it was trained and evaluated on.

The Pareto Cut Your eval score is a measurement taken at one point in time, on one snapshot of data. It decays. The Pareto move is simple: monitor your production input distribution against your training/eval distribution on an ongoing basis, don't just eval once and assume it holds forever.
Where It Shows Up This is the actual statistical explanation for why a model that scored great in your eval quietly gets worse over months in production - user behavior, input phrasing, or the underlying task itself drifts away from what you originally measured.
Learn From Chip Huyen's "Designing Machine Learning Systems" - the drift-detection chapter specifically is the best treatment of this available.
