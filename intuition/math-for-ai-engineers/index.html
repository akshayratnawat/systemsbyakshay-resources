The Only Math You Need to Become an AI Engineer

Not the math you took in college. The math that's actually running while your model trains - explained via the 20% of each concept that does 80% of the work.

Most "math for ML" content stops at linear algebra and basic gradient descent, then assumes you'll pick up the rest from papers. This is the next tier up: the calculus and optimization-theory concepts that explain what's actually happening when a model trains, why it sometimes plateaus, and why certain lines of code are written the way they are. Same Pareto-cut approach as the statistics companion post - the small slice of each concept that explains almost everything you'll touch.

1. The Chain Rule

The rule for finding the derivative of a function made of nested functions - and the single mathematical fact that makes backpropagation possible.

The Pareto Cut You don't need to derive it. You need the picture: to know how much a change deep inside a hundred-layer network affects the final loss, you multiply local derivatives together, one layer at a time, working backward from the output.
The Idea
dL/dw₁ = dL/dy · dy/dz · dz/dw₁
Where It Shows Up Every time a framework "calls .backward()," it is running the chain rule millions of times per training step. It is not an abstraction sitting behind PyTorch - it is the entire mechanism PyTorch is automating for you.
Learn From 3Blue1Brown's "Backpropagation calculus"; Karpathy's micrograd build, where he implements this rule from scratch on camera.

2. The Jacobian Matrix

The generalization of "the derivative" to functions that map multiple inputs to multiple outputs.

The Pareto Cut A single derivative answers "how much does the one output change per unit of input." A Jacobian answers the same question for every output, with respect to every input, all at once - it's a full matrix of those answers instead of one number.
Where It Shows Up Almost every layer in a neural network maps a vector to another vector, which means almost every layer has a Jacobian. Backpropagation through a deep network is really the chain rule applied to a sequence of Jacobians, not single-number derivatives.
Learn From The Jacobian/Hessian intuition writeups on Medium and GeeksforGeeks are solid for building the visual picture without getting lost in notation.

3. Taylor Series

A way of approximating any smooth function, near a specific point, using a sum of simpler polynomial terms.

The Pareto Cut You need exactly the first-order term. It says: if you zoom in close enough on any smooth loss function, it looks approximately linear - and the gradient is that local linear approximation. This is the actual justification for why a small gradient-descent step moves you in a useful direction, not a hack that happens to work.
Where It Shows Up Every gradient descent update is implicitly trusting a first-order Taylor approximation of the loss around your current parameters. Go one term further in the expansion and you get the piece that describes curvature - which is the Hessian, next.
Learn From Any standard calculus course's Taylor series unit - then deliberately re-read the gradient descent update rule with this lens afterward.

4. The Hessian Matrix & Convexity

The matrix of second derivatives - it describes the curvature of your loss function, not just its slope.

The Pareto Cut One classification is enough to be useful: a positive-definite Hessian means you're near a genuine minimum (the bowl curves up in every direction - convex, easy). An indefinite Hessian means you're at a saddle point - it curves up in some directions and down in others, so it only looks like a minimum from certain angles.
Where It Shows Up This is the actual reason Adam and SGD (which only use first-order gradient information) are cheap and dominant at LLM scale, while Newton's method and quasi-Newton methods (which use the Hessian directly) converge in fewer steps but are far too expensive to compute at billions of parameters.
Learn From DataCamp's Hessian matrix tutorial; Cadence's "All About the Hessian Matrix, Convexity, and Optimization" writeup.

5. Local Minima vs. Saddle Points

The geometric consequence of the Hessian classification above - what "training got stuck" actually looks like, geometrically.

The Pareto Cut In the very high-dimensional loss landscapes LLMs train in, true local minima are rare and saddle points are common - a point that curves down in most directions but up in a few. "Training plateaued" is much more often "crossing a saddle" than "trapped at the bottom of a bowl."
Where It Shows Up This reframes what a loss plateau means during training - it changes how you'd reason about learning-rate schedules, warmup, and momentum-based optimizers, all of which exist partly to help escape saddle regions rather than to find a "better" minimum.
Learn From The same Hessian resources above - the geometry falls out directly once the Hessian classification clicks.

6. Log-Sum-Exp / Working in Log-Space

Why nearly every ML codebase computes log-probabilities instead of raw probabilities.

The Pareto Cut Multiplying many small probabilities together underflows to zero on real hardware almost immediately. Adding their logarithms together doesn't. That's the entire reason - not elegance, numerical survival.
Where It Shows Up This is the actual reason behind half the "log" prefixes scattered through loss functions and codebases - log-likelihood, log-softmax, log-sum-exp as a stable building block. Once you know why, these stop looking like arbitrary naming conventions.
Learn From Search "log-sum-exp trick numerical stability" alongside whatever deep learning framework docs you're already using - most frameworks document their own stable implementations directly.

7. Matrix Calculus Notation

Just enough fluency in derivative notation for vectors and matrices to read a paper's gradient derivation without stalling out.

The Pareto Cut You don't need to derive matrix calculus results yourself. You need pattern recognition: spotting a gradient vector, a Jacobian, and a Hessian on sight, and knowing roughly what shape each one is (vector, matrix, matrix) relative to the function they came from.
Where It Shows Up This is the bridge back to linear algebra - the deep linear algebra ground (vectors, matmul, eigenvectors, SVD) is covered in a companion piece; this is specifically the notation fluency needed to read *derivations*, not just operations.
Learn From The "Matrix Calculus for Deep Learning" reference sheets that circulate widely (search the exact phrase) - most people bookmark one rather than memorize it, which is the right call.
